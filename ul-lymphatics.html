Lymphatic Drainage of the Upper Limb

Easy and long study notes covering lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes, and clinical importance.

Overview Lymphatic vessels Lymph nodes Drainage pathway Clinical points Quick revision

Chapter Overview

The lymphatic system of the upper limb drains tissue fluid, proteins, and waste products back into the bloodstream. It also helps the body fight infection.

In the upper limb, lymphatic drainage follows the same general regional pattern as the blood vessels: hand, forearm, arm, and shoulder.

Most lymph from the upper limb eventually passes to the axillary lymph nodes and then into the subclavian lymph trunk.

Lymphatic Vessels

Superficial Lymphatic Vessels

These vessels lie in the skin and subcutaneous tissue.

They commonly follow the superficial veins of the upper limb, especially the cephalic vein and basilic vein.

Deep Lymphatic Vessels

These vessels travel deep in the limb and usually accompany the arteries.

They drain muscles, bones, joints, and deeper soft tissues.

Both superficial and deep lymphatic channels communicate with each other, so lymph can move through multiple pathways before reaching the lymph nodes.

Lymph Nodes of the Upper Limb

1. Cubital Nodes

Cubital lymph nodes are found near the elbow, especially in the cubital fossa or just above the medial epicondyle.

They receive lymph from the medial hand and forearm.

2. Brachial Nodes

These nodes lie along the brachial vessels in the arm.

They receive lymph from the deep tissues of the arm.

3. Deltopectoral Nodes

These nodes are located near the cephalic vein in the deltopectoral groove.

They help drain lymph from the lateral upper limb.

4. Axillary Nodes

These are the most important lymph nodes of the upper limb.

They receive lymph from the upper limb, chest wall, and shoulder region.

Axillary lymph node groups:

  • Pectoral nodes
  • Subscapular nodes
  • Humeral nodes
  • Central nodes
  • Apical nodes

Drainage Pathway

Lymph from the hand and forearm first enters superficial and deep lymphatic vessels.

Some lymph passes to the cubital nodes, while most of it travels upward through the arm to the axillary nodes.

Lymph from the axillary nodes then passes to the apical axillary nodes, forms the subclavian lymph trunk, and finally drains into the venous system.

Region Main drainage route
Hand Superficial and deep lymphatic vessels
Forearm Cubital nodes and brachial nodes
Arm Brachial nodes and axillary nodes
Shoulder Axillary nodes
Final drainage Subclavian lymph trunk → thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct

Clinical Importance

Lymphedema

If lymph drainage is blocked, fluid collects in the tissues and causes swelling called lymphedema.

Infection Spread

Lymph nodes may enlarge when they fight infection in the hand, forearm, arm, or shoulder.

Breast and Axillary Nodes

Because the axillary nodes also receive lymph from the chest wall and breast, they are important in clinical examination and surgery.

Sentinel Nodes

Doctors may examine lymph nodes to check if cancer or infection has spread through lymphatic channels.

Quick Revision

  • Superficial lymph vessels follow superficial veins.
  • Deep lymph vessels follow deep arteries.
  • Cubital nodes are near the elbow.
  • Brachial nodes are in the arm.
  • Axillary nodes are the most important nodes of the upper limb.
  • Final drainage goes to the subclavian lymph trunk.
  • Lymphatic blockage can cause swelling called lymphedema.
