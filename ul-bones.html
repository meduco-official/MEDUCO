Bones of the Upper Limb

The upper limb skeleton has 32 bones on each side. They are grouped region by region: shoulder girdle, arm, forearm, and hand. Below is a detailed, exam-focused look at each bone — the kind of detail useful for FMGE and other competitive medical exams.

A. Clavicle (Collar Bone)

The clavicle is the only long bone in the body that lies horizontally and is the first bone to start ossifying in the embryo (around the 5th–6th week), yet it is one of the last bones to complete ossification (fuses around 21–25 years — its medial end is the last epiphysis to fuse in the whole body). This fact is a favourite exam point.

Shape and parts:

  • S-shaped bone: medial two-thirds is convex forward, lateral one-third is concave forward
  • Two ends: sternal (medial) end — large, quadrangular, articulates with the manubrium of sternum; and acromial (lateral) end — flat, articulates with the acromion of scapula
  • No medullary cavity in adults (it is made of cancellous bone with a thin cortical shell) — this is why it is a common fracture site

Important attachments (exam favourites):

  • Subclavius muscle attaches to a groove on the lower surface
  • Conoid tubercle and trapezoid line on the lower surface of the lateral third — attachment of the coracoclavicular ligament (conoid and trapezoid parts), important in acromioclavicular joint injuries
  • Costoclavicular ligament attaches to a rough impression near the sternal end
  • Muscles attaching: sternocleidomastoid and pectoralis major (medial part), deltoid and trapezius (lateral part)

Clinical relevance:

  • Most commonly fractured bone in the body, usually at the junction of the middle and lateral thirds (the weakest point, where the two curves meet and where the bone changes from tubular to flattened shape)
  • The medial fragment is pulled upward by sternocleidomastoid, and the lateral fragment is pulled downward by the weight of the limb (and pectoralis major pulls it medially) — this is a classic FMGE-type question
  • Acts as a strut holding the upper limb away from the trunk, protecting the underlying brachial plexus and subclavian vessels

B. Scapula (Shoulder Blade)

A flat, triangular bone lying over the back of the ribs (2nd to 7th rib level), with two surfaces, three borders, three angles, and three processes.

Surfaces:

  • Costal (anterior) surface: has the subscapular fossa, occupied by subscapularis muscle
  • Dorsal (posterior) surface: divided by the spine of scapula into the supraspinous fossa (supraspinatus) and infraspinous fossa (infraspinatus)
  • Borders: superior, lateral (axillary), and medial (vertebral)
  • Angles: superior angle, inferior angle, and lateral angle (which bears the glenoid cavity — the shallow socket for the shoulder joint)

Processes:

  • Acromion process: flat process continuing from the spine, articulates with the clavicle at the acromioclavicular joint
  • Coracoid process: a finger-like projection important because THREE structures attach to its tip: pectoralis minor inserts here, while coracobrachialis and the short head of biceps brachii originate from here (mnemonic often used: "MCL")
  • Spine of scapula
  • Glenoid cavity: shallow, pear-shaped, faces laterally; its shallow depth is why the shoulder joint is so mobile but also prone to dislocation. It is deepened slightly by the glenoid labrum (fibrocartilage rim).

Muscle attachments (high FMGE yield):

  • Suprascapular notch: transmits the suprascapular nerve below the superior transverse scapular ligament (nerve passes below, artery passes above — classic exam point)
  • Spinoglenoid notch: transmits the suprascapular nerve and vessels to the infraspinous fossa

Clinical relevance:

  • Scapula fractures are less common because it is well protected by surrounding muscles (needs high-force trauma)
  • "Winging of scapula" occurs when the long thoracic nerve (supplying serratus anterior) is damaged — the medial border and inferior angle stick out backward, especially when pushing against a wall

C. Humerus

The longest and largest bone of the upper limb, extending from the shoulder to the elbow.

Upper end:

  • Head: rounded, faces medially, backward, and upward; articulates with the glenoid cavity
  • Anatomical neck: separates head from tubercles
  • Greater tubercle (lateral) and Lesser tubercle (medial): separated by the intertubercular (bicipital) groove, which transmits the tendon of the long head of biceps brachii
  • Surgical neck: junction of upper end and shaft; a common fracture site because it is narrow, and the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery wind around it here — fracture at this point can cause axillary nerve injury (loss of deltoid function, "flattened shoulder," and sensory loss over the "regimental badge" area)

Rotator cuff insertions on the tubercles (very high yield):

  • Greater tubercle: from above downward: Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor (mnemonic: "SIT")
  • Lesser tubercle: Subscapularis
  • (Teres major inserts on the crest of the lesser tubercle, not on the tubercle itself)

Shaft:

  • Deltoid tuberosity on the lateral surface — insertion of deltoid
  • Spiral (radial) groove on the posterior surface — transmits the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery; a mid-shaft humerus fracture here classically causes radial nerve palsy → wrist drop, another very common exam scenario
  • Nutrient foramen usually directed toward the elbow

Lower end:

  • Capitulum (lateral, rounded, articulates with radius) and Trochlea (medial, pulley-shaped, articulates with ulna)
  • Medial epicondyle: larger, gives origin to the common flexor origin (flexor-pronator muscles of forearm); the ulnar nerve passes directly behind it (superficial and easily injured here — "funny bone" area, causes tingling in the little and ring fingers)
  • Lateral epicondyle: gives origin to the common extensor origin
  • Coronoid fossa, radial fossa (anteriorly), Olecranon fossa (posteriorly) — accommodate the corresponding processes of ulna and radius during elbow movements

Clinical relevance:

  • Supracondylar fracture of humerus (common in children after a fall on outstretched hand) can injure the brachial artery and median nerve, risking Volkmann's ischemic contracture — a classic FMGE topic
  • Fracture of medial epicondyle can damage the ulnar nerve

D. Radius

The lateral bone of the forearm, shorter than the ulna, and the main bone contributing to the wrist joint.

Upper end:

  • Head: disc-shaped, articulates with the capitulum of humerus and the radial notch of ulna
  • Neck
  • Radial tuberosity: insertion of biceps brachii tendon

Shaft:

  • Has an interosseous border (medial side) for attachment of the interosseous membrane, connecting it to the ulna
  • Pronator teres inserts on the lateral surface of the shaft

Lower end (much larger than the upper end — opposite of ulna):

  • Styloid process on the lateral side
  • Ulnar notch medially, for articulation with the head of ulna
  • Dorsal tubercle (of Lister): a groove medial to it transmits the tendon of extensor pollicis longus, used as a surgical landmark
  • Articulates below with the scaphoid and lunate bones to form the wrist joint

Clinical relevance:

  • Colles' fracture: fracture of the lower end of radius (within 2.5 cm of the wrist), typically from a fall on an outstretched hand, producing the classic "dinner fork" deformity
  • Fracture of the radial neck can be associated with posterior interosseous nerve injury

E. Ulna

The medial bone of the forearm, longer than the radius, and the main bone contributing to the elbow joint.

Upper end (much larger than the lower end — opposite of radius):

  • Olecranon process: forms the point of the elbow, receives insertion of triceps brachii
  • Coronoid process: projects forward, has the radial notch on its lateral side (for head of radius) and the tuberosity of ulna below (insertion of brachialis)
  • Trochlear notch: large articular surface for the trochlea of humerus

Shaft & Lower end:

  • Interosseous border laterally for the interosseous membrane
  • Head: rounded, articulates with the ulnar notch of radius
  • Styloid process projects downward from the back of the head
  • Note: the head of ulna does not directly articulate with the carpal bones — it is separated by an articular disc (triangular fibrocartilage complex)

Clinical relevance:

  • Monteggia fracture-dislocation: fracture of the upper third of the ulna shaft with dislocation of the head of radius; a well-known FMGE pairing
  • Olecranon fractures happen from direct falls onto the elbow and can affect triceps function

F. Bones of the Hand

The hand has three groups of bones, totaling 27 bones (more than the rest of the entire upper limb combined):

1. Carpal bones (8 bones, in two rows)

  • Proximal row (lateral to medial): Scaphoid, Lunate, Triquetrum, Pisiform
  • Distal row (lateral to medial): Trapezium, Trapezoid, Capitate, Hamate
  • Mnemonic: "She Looks Too Pretty, Try To Catch Her"
  • Scaphoid: most commonly fractured carpal bone; poor blood supply in its proximal part makes it prone to avascular necrosis after fracture — a very important FMGE point
  • Lunate: most commonly dislocated carpal bone
  • Pisiform: a sesamoid bone (develops within the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris)
  • Hook of hamate: can fracture in sports involving bats/clubs (like golf) and can injure the ulnar nerve at Guyon's canal

2. Metacarpal bones (5 bones)

Numbered I to V from thumb to little finger. Each has a base, shaft, and head (forms the "knuckle"). Fracture of the neck of the 5th metacarpal from punching is called a "Boxer's fracture."

3. Phalanges (14 bones)

The thumb has only 2 phalanges (proximal and distal), while each of the other four fingers has 3 (proximal, middle, distal).
