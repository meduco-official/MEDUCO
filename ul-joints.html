Joints of the Upper Limb

Before studying the individual joints of the upper limb, it is essential to understand what a joint is, why joints are important, and how they are classified. Once these basic concepts are clear, learning the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand joints becomes much easier.

What is a Joint?

A joint, also known as an articulation (from the Latin articulare, meaning "to join" or "to fit together"), is the place where two or more bones meet. The primary purpose of a joint is to connect bones while allowing varying degrees of movement—from completely immovable to a wide range of motion.

Why Are Joints Important?

Without joints, the human skeleton would be a rigid framework incapable of movement. Joints enable walking, running, jumping, lifting, writing, eating, speaking, breathing, maintaining posture, and shock absorption.

Components That Form a Joint

  • Bones: Provide the rigid framework (e.g., humerus, radius, ulna).
  • Cartilage: Covers articular surfaces and reduces friction.
  • Ligaments: Connect bone to bone and stabilize joints.
  • Joint Capsule & Synovial Membrane: Enclose synovial joints and produce lubricating synovial fluid.
  • Muscles and Tendons: Provide movement and additional dynamic stability.
  • Bursae & Accessory Structures: Reduce friction (bursae) and improve joint congruity (discs, menisci, labrum).

Classification of Joints

Joints are classified in two major ways:

1. Structural Classification

  • Fibrous Joints: Connected by dense fibrous tissue; no joint cavity; movement is absent or minimal (e.g., sutures, interosseous membrane between radius and ulna).
  • Cartilaginous Joints: Connected by cartilage; no joint cavity; slight movement (e.g., epiphyseal plates, symphyses).
  • Synovial Joints: Separated by a fluid-filled joint cavity; allow free movement. Most upper limb joints belong to this type.

2. Functional Classification

  • Synarthrosis: Immovable joints (e.g., skull sutures).
  • Amphiarthrosis: Slightly movable joints (e.g., pubic symphysis).
  • Diarthrosis: Freely movable joints (e.g., shoulder, elbow, wrist).

Deep Dive: Synovial Joints

Synovial joints are the most common, most movable, and most complex joints in the upper limb (shoulder, elbow, wrist, thumb, fingers, and radioulnar joints). Every typical synovial joint possesses:

  • Articular surfaces covered by hyaline cartilage
  • A joint cavity filled with slippery synovial fluid
  • An articular capsule consisting of a fibrous outer layer for strength and an inner synovial membrane
  • Reinforcing ligaments and a rich nerve and blood supply

Blood Supply, Nerve Supply, and Hilton’s Law

1. Blood Supply

Articular cartilage is avascular (lacks blood vessels) and receives its nutrition via diffusion from the synovial fluid. The surrounding fibrous capsule, synovial membrane, and ligaments are richly vascularized from surrounding articular branches to support tissue repair and fluid production.

2. Nerve Supply & Hilton’s Law

Joint capsules and synovial membranes contain abundant sensory nerve endings responsible for pain and proprioception (joint position sense). According to Hilton’s Law: The nerve supplying a joint also supplies the muscles that move that joint and the skin covering the attachments of those muscles. This anatomical rule explains why deep joint irritation can result in referred pain around nearby muscle regions.

Joint Stability vs. Mobility

A functional balance between mobility and stability is maintained by:

  • Bony architecture: Shape and depth of the articulating surfaces.
  • Capsule and Ligaments: Prevent hyperextension, hyperflexion, and dislocation.
  • Muscles and Tendons: Provide dynamic stabilization during active movement.
  • Accessory Structures: Labra, menisci, and articular discs that deepen sockets and distribute load evenly.

Clinical Correlations

  • Osteoarthritis: Wear-and-tear degeneration of articular cartilage causing pain and stiffness.
  • Rheumatoid Arthritis: Autoimmune inflammation of the synovial membrane.
  • Joint Effusion & Synovitis: Abnormal accumulation of fluid or inflammation within the joint cavity.
  • Dislocations: Displacement of articular surfaces due to capsular/ligamentous failure (e.g., highly mobile shoulder joints are prone to dislocation).
