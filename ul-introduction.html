Upper Limb Anatomy – A Complete Simple Guide

Introduction

The upper limb is the part of our body that includes the shoulder, arm, forearm, wrist, and hand. It starts from where the arm joins the trunk (body) and ends at the fingertips. This is one of the most important parts of the human body because it helps us do almost every daily activity — writing, eating, lifting, holding, throwing, and even simple things like scratching an itch.

Doctors, medical students, and physiotherapists study the upper limb very carefully because it is a common site of injuries, fractures, and nerve problems. Understanding its structure helps in diagnosing diseases, treating injuries, and performing surgeries correctly.

Why the Upper Limb Is Special

Unlike the lower limb, which is mainly built for standing, walking, and carrying body weight, the upper limb is built for movement and fine control. It is not a weight-bearing part of the body (except in special cases like using crutches or doing push-ups). Because of this, the upper limb has:

  • More freedom of movement compared to the lower limb
  • Joints that can move in many directions (like the shoulder joint, which is the most movable joint in the body)
  • Muscles that allow both power (like lifting heavy things) and precision (like threading a needle)
  • A rich network of nerves and blood vessels, since the hand needs very fine and accurate control

Regions of the Upper Limb

To study the upper limb easily, doctors divide it into four main regions:

  • Shoulder region – Connects the arm to the trunk. It includes the shoulder joint and the muscles around it (like the deltoid and rotator cuff muscles). This region gives the arm its wide range of motion.
  • Arm (Brachium) – The part between the shoulder and the elbow. It contains the humerus bone and muscles that bend and straighten the elbow.
  • Forearm (Antebrachium) – The part between the elbow and the wrist. It has two bones, the radius and the ulna, and many muscles that move the wrist and fingers.
  • Hand (Manus) – The most complex region, made up of small bones, joints, muscles, and tendons that allow gripping, holding, and delicate finger movements.

How the Upper Limb Connects to the Body

The upper limb is attached to the trunk through the shoulder girdle (also called the pectoral girdle). This girdle is made of two bones:

  • Clavicle (collar bone) – a long bone in front, shaped like an "S"
  • Scapula (shoulder blade) – a flat, triangular bone at the back

These two bones do not directly attach to the spine with a strong joint. Instead, they are held in place mainly by muscles. This is why the shoulder has such a huge range of movement compared to the hip joint in the lower limb, which is deeply fixed into the pelvis for stability.

Main Systems Inside the Upper Limb

When we study the upper limb, we usually look at it through different systems, each with its own importance:

  • Skeletal system – the bones that give shape and support (clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpal bones, metacarpals, phalanges)
  • Joints – the places where bones meet and allow movement (shoulder, elbow, wrist, and small joints of the hand)
  • Muscular system – muscles that produce movement, grouped by region (shoulder muscles, arm muscles, forearm muscles, hand muscles)
  • Nervous system – mainly supplied by the brachial plexus, a network of nerves that starts from the neck and spreads down into the whole upper limb, controlling movement and sensation
  • Blood supply – arteries bring oxygen-rich blood (starting from the subclavian artery, continuing as axillary, then brachial, then splitting into radial and ulnar arteries), and veins carry blood back to the heart
  • Lymphatic system – helps drain fluid and fight infection, with important lymph node groups in the armpit (axilla)

Clinical Importance

The upper limb is frequently involved in real-life medical situations, which is why studying it in detail is so useful:

  • Fractures of the clavicle, humerus, radius, and ulna are very common, especially after falls or accidents
  • Nerve injuries (like damage to the radial, median, ulnar, or axillary nerve) can cause specific problems such as wrist drop, claw hand, or loss of sensation in certain areas
  • Shoulder dislocations happen often because the shoulder joint is so mobile, which makes it less stable
  • Blood vessel injuries in the arm can affect circulation to the entire hand
  • Understanding surface landmarks (like where to feel a pulse or give an injection) depends on knowing the anatomy underneath
